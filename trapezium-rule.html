Trapezium Rule.

Estimating the area under a curve by replacing sections of the curve with straight-line approximations.

Introduction

One of the uses of integration is to find the area under a curve. However, in some cases, finding the exact area using integration may not be possible or may be difficult to evaluate directly. In such situations, we can use an estimation method to approximate the area.

One of the methods used to estimate the area under a curve is the Trapezium Rule.

Estimating the Area

Consider the curve y = f(x), and suppose that we want to find the area between the curve and the x-axis from x = a to x = b.

To estimate this area, we divide the interval from a to b into n equal intervals.

Let the width of each interval be h. Since the total width of the interval is b − a, and it has been divided into n equal parts:

h = b − a n

We can therefore mark the x-coordinates as:

x₀ = a     x₁     x₂     x₃     ...     xₙ = b

Because the intervals are equal, the distance between every two consecutive x-coordinates is the same and is equal to h.

The Curve and the Intervals

At each of these x-coordinates, we can find the corresponding point on the curve. Let the corresponding y-coordinates be y₀, y₁, y₂, ... , yₙ, where:

yᵢ = f(xᵢ)

Therefore, each point on the curve can be written in the form (xᵢ, yᵢ).

Replacing the Curve

The curve between two consecutive points is not a straight line. Therefore, finding the exact area of each small section may still be difficult.

Instead, we approximate each small section of the curve by drawing a straight line between two consecutive points.

For example, consider the two consecutive points (x₀, y₀) and (x₁, y₁).

We draw a straight line joining these two points. Together with the x-axis and the two vertical lines at x₀ and x₁, this forms a trapezium.

The parallel sides of this trapezium have lengths y₀ and y₁, while its width is h.

A Series of Trapezia

We can repeat the same process between every pair of consecutive points.

Therefore, the region under the curve is replaced by a series of trapezia.

The total area of these trapezia gives us an estimate of the area under the original curve.

Why Is It an Approximation?

The straight lines do not follow the curve exactly. Therefore, there will usually be a difference between the actual area under the curve and the area of the trapezia.

However, as we increase the number of intervals, each interval becomes narrower. The straight-line approximations then follow the curve more closely, making the estimated area closer to the actual area.

The main idea Divide the interval into equal parts, replace each section of the curve with a straight line, calculate the area of each resulting trapezium, and add them together.

The Trapezium Rule Formula

We now have the basic idea behind the Trapezium Rule. Each section of the curve is replaced by a trapezium, and the areas of all the trapezia are added together.

To develop the formula, we first need to recall the area of a trapezium.

Area of One Trapezium

The area of a trapezium is given by:

A = 1 2 × (sum of parallel sides) × (perpendicular distance)

For the first trapezium, the parallel sides have lengths y₀ and y₁, and the width is h.

A₁ = 1 2 h(y₀ + y₁)

Adding the Trapezia

The second trapezium has parallel sides y₁ and y₂. Therefore:

A₂ = 1 2 h(y₁ + y₂)

If we have two trapezia, the total estimated area is:

A ≈ h 2 (y₀ + y₁) + h 2 (y₁ + y₂)

Taking the common factor h/2 outside:

A ≈ h 2 (y₀ + 2y₁ + y₂)
Notice the pattern The first and last y-values appear once, while the values between them appear twice.

For n Trapezia

If the interval from a to b is divided into n equal intervals, there will be n trapezia.

Adding all of their areas gives the Trapezium Rule:

∫ a to b f(x) dx ≈ h 2 [ y₀ + yₙ + 2(y₁ + y₂ + ... + yₙ₋₁) ]
The Trapezium Rule Add the first and last y-values once, add all the intermediate y-values twice, then multiply the entire result by h/2.
