Polar Form

We already know how to describe a complex number using its real and imaginary parts. Now we’ll describe the same point using its distance from the origin and the angle it makes.

A new way to describe the same number
rθ
01

One point, two descriptions

A complex number \(z=x+iy\) can be plotted as the point \((x,y)\) on the complex plane. Instead of describing the point by its horizontal and vertical coordinates, we can describe it by two measurements.

1. Modulus \(r\)
The distance from the origin to the point. Think: how far away is the complex number?

2. Argument \(\theta\)
The angle measured from the positive real axis to the line joining the origin to the point.

The modulus is a distance, so it cannot be negative. The argument is an angle, and its value depends on the direction you choose to measure.
\[r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\]

Because the point forms a right-angled triangle, Pythagoras gives us the modulus. The angle tells us the direction of the point.

02

From Cartesian to polar form

Start with \(z=x+iy\). The horizontal coordinate is \(x\), and the vertical coordinate is \(y\). Trigonometry connects these coordinates to the modulus and argument.

Horizontal component
\[x=r\cos\theta\]

Cosine gives the horizontal part of the radius.

Vertical component
\[y=r\sin\theta\]

Sine gives the vertical part of the radius.

Put the two components back together

Replace \(x\) and \(y\) in \(z=x+iy\):

\[\boxed{z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)}\]

This is the polar form of a complex number. It is the same complex number, simply written using its modulus and argument.

Worked example · Cartesian to polar

Write \(z=3+3i\) in polar form, using an exact angle.

Find the modulus.
\(r=\sqrt{3^2+3^2}=\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2\).

Find the argument.
The real and imaginary parts are equal and positive, so the point is in the first quadrant. Its angle is \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\).

Write the polar form.
Use the modulus and angle in the polar-form template.

Result
\[z=3\sqrt2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\]
Angle check: Always use the position of the point to identify its quadrant. The ratio \(y/x\) alone does not tell you the quadrant. In this lesson, angles are in radians unless a question says otherwise.
03

Exponential form

There is a shorter way to write polar form. Euler’s relation connects the trigonometric expression to an exponential expression.

\[\cos\theta+i\sin\theta=e^{i\theta}\]

So we can replace the bracket in polar form with \(e^{i\theta}\):

\[\boxed{z=re^{i\theta}}\]

This is called exponential form. It does not describe a different kind of number. It is simply another notation for the same polar form.

Polar form
\[r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\]

Useful when working directly with sine and cosine.

Exponential form
\[re^{i\theta}\]

A compact notation that makes the product and quotient rules easy to see.

04

Multiplying in polar form

Suppose two complex numbers are already written in polar form. Multiplying them follows a wonderfully tidy pattern.

Let \(z_1=r_1(\cos\theta_1+i\sin\theta_1)\) and \(z_2=r_2(\cos\theta_2+i\sin\theta_2)\).

\[\boxed{|z_1z_2|=r_1r_2}\qquad\boxed{\arg(z_1z_2)=\theta_1+\theta_2}\]

In plain language:

×

Multiply the moduli

Multiply the two distances to get the new distance.

＋

Add the arguments

Add the two angles to get the new direction.

Worked example · Product

Let \(z_1=4(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{6})\) and \(z_2=3(\cos\frac{\pi}{3}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{3})\). Find \(z_1z_2\).

Modulus: \(4\times3=12\).

Argument: \(\frac{\pi}{6}+\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{\pi}{2}\).

Result
\[z_1z_2=12\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{2}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\]
The same rule is especially quick in exponential form: \(r_1e^{i\theta_1}\times r_2e^{i\theta_2}=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\). The coefficients multiply, while the powers combine by adding their exponents.
05

Dividing in polar form

Division uses the reverse angle operation. The modulus is divided, and the argument of the denominator is subtracted.

\[\boxed{\left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right|=\frac{r_1}{r_2}}\qquad\boxed{\arg\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)=\theta_1-\theta_2}\]
÷

Divide the moduli

Divide the numerator’s modulus by the denominator’s modulus.

−

Subtract the arguments

Top angle minus bottom angle. Keep the order clear.

Worked example · Quotient

Using \(z_1=10(\cos\frac{2\pi}{7}+i\sin\frac{2\pi}{7})\) and \(z_2=2(\cos\frac{9\pi}{14}+i\sin\frac{9\pi}{14})\), find \(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\).

Modulus: \(\frac{10}{2}=5\).

Argument: \(\frac{2\pi}{7}-\frac{9\pi}{14}=\frac{4\pi}{14}-\frac{9\pi}{14}=-\frac{5\pi}{14}\).

Result
\[\frac{z_1}{z_2}=5\left(\cos\left(-\frac{5\pi}{14}\right)+i\sin\left(-\frac{5\pi}{14}\right)\right)\]
Division by zero is not defined, so the denominator’s modulus must not be zero.
06

The whole lesson in a minute

✓Modulus: \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), the distance from the origin.
✓Argument: \(\theta\), the angle measured from the positive real axis.
✓Polar form: \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\).
✓Exponential form: \(z=re^{i\theta}\).
✓Multiplication: multiply moduli, add arguments.
✓Division: divide moduli, subtract arguments.
07

Common mistakes to avoid

Watch out for these common errors when working with polar form.

! Mixing up sine and cosine. Remember that x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ.
! Choosing the wrong quadrant. Always check the signs of the real and imaginary parts.
! Forgetting that modulus is non-negative. A modulus represents distance, so it cannot be negative.
! Adding arguments during division. Subtract the denominator's argument instead.
! Reversing the subtraction. Always calculate θ₁ − θ₂.
! Mixing degrees and radians. Keep the angle unit consistent throughout your calculation.
! Ignoring the principal argument. Check the required argument interval in the question.
