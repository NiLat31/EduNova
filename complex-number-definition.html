Definition of a Complex Number.

Step beyond the real number line. Discover the imaginary unit, simplify powers of \(i\), represent complex numbers on the Argand diagram, and explore their modulus and argument.

LESSON 9.1

Two parts. One new number system.

Start with the imaginary unit and the algebraic form. Then move to the geometry of complex numbers through modulus and argument.

! PAY ATTENTION

Degrees are used in Vectors, while radians are used in Complex Numbers. In this lesson, measure arguments in radians unless a question specifically asks for degrees.

01
PART ONE

Definition of Complex Numbers

From the real number line to the complex plane.

1. Why do we need complex numbers?

Consider the quadratic equation:

\[x^2+6x+13=0\]

Using the discriminant,

\[\Delta=b^2-4ac=6^2-4(1)(13)=36-52=-16\]

The discriminant is negative. Therefore, the equation has no real roots because the square root of a negative real number is not a real number.

To extend our number system and solve equations like this, we introduce a new number called the imaginary unit.

2. The imaginary unit

The imaginary unit, denoted by \(i\), is defined by:

\[\boxed{i=\sqrt{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad i^2=-1}\]

This allows us to express square roots of negative numbers using \(i\).

Example 1

\(\sqrt{-49}\)

\[\sqrt{-49}=\sqrt{49}\sqrt{-1}=\boxed{7i}\]

Example 2

\(\sqrt{-16}\)

\[\sqrt{-16}=\sqrt{16}\sqrt{-1}=\boxed{4i}\]
! IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

The principal square root is \(\sqrt{-49}=7i\). However, the equation \(z^2=-49\) has two solutions: \(z=\pm7i\). Do not confuse a square-root symbol with solving a quadratic equation.

3. Simplifying expressions involving \(i\)

Before simplifying longer expressions, notice what happens when we calculate the first four powers of \(i\):

\[i^1=i\]
\[i^2=-1\]
\[i^3=i^2\cdot i=-i\]
\[i^4=i^2\cdot i^2=1\]

After the fourth power, the same pattern repeats because \(i^4=1\).

\[i,\quad -1,\quad -i,\quad 1,\quad i,\quad -1,\quad -i,\quad 1,\ldots\]

To simplify a large power of \(i\), divide its exponent by 4 and use the remainder.

Decimal part Remainder Resulting power
.000\(i^0=1\)
.251\(i^1=i\)
.502\(i^2=-1\)
.753\(i^3=-i\)
R REMEMBER

When multiplying powers with the same base, we add their exponents:

\[a^m\times a^n=a^{m+n}\]

Therefore, \(i^{2024}\times i^2=i^{2024+2}=i^{2026}\). This explains why we can split the exponent into \(2024+2\).

Example 1: Simplify \(i^{2026}\)

1

Divide the exponent by 4.

\[2026\div4=506.5\]
2

The decimal part is .50, so the remainder is 2.

\[2026=2024+2\]
3

Use \(i^4=1\).

\[\begin{aligned} i^{2026}&=(i^4)^{506}i^2\\ &=1^{506}(-1)\\ &=\boxed{-1} \end{aligned}\]

Example 2: Simplify \(i^{537}\)

1

Divide the exponent by 4.

\[537\div4=134.25\]
2

The decimal part is .25, so the remainder is 1.

\[537=536+1\]
3

Use \(i^4=1\).

\[\begin{aligned} i^{537}&=(i^4)^{134}i\\ &=1^{134}i\\ &=\boxed{i} \end{aligned}\]
! CHECK THE REMAINDER

Because the exponent is an integer, dividing it by 4 can only give decimal parts .00, .25, .50 or .75. Match the remainder carefully with the table before simplifying.

4. Real and imaginary parts

A complex number is written in the form:

\[\boxed{z=x+iy}\]

where \(x\) and \(y\) are real numbers, and \(i\) is the imaginary unit.

Real part

The real part is the value of \(x\).

\[\operatorname{Re}(z)=x\]

Imaginary part

The imaginary part is the coefficient of \(i\), which is \(y\).

\[\operatorname{Im}(z)=y\]

Example

For \(z=5-3i\):

\[\operatorname{Re}(z)=5,\qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=-3\]

Notice that the imaginary part is \(-3\), not \(-3i\).

5. Equality of complex numbers

Two complex numbers are equal if and only if their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal.

\[a+bi=c+di\iff a=c\quad\text{and}\quad b=d\]

Example

Given that \(3x+(y-2)i=9+5i\), find \(x\) and \(y\).

\[\begin{aligned}3x&=9\\x&=\boxed{3}\end{aligned} \qquad \begin{aligned}y-2&=5\\y&=\boxed{7}\end{aligned}\]

6. The Argand diagram

A complex number can be represented geometrically as a point on a plane called the Argand diagram (or complex plane).

  • The horizontal axis represents the real part.
  • The vertical axis represents the imaginary part.
  • The complex number \(z=x+iy\) is represented by the point \((x,y)\).
The point representing \(z=3+2i\) has coordinates \((3,2)\).
✓ KEY INFORMATION • PART 1

Complex number: \(z=x+iy\), where \(x,y\in\mathbb R\) and \(i^2=-1\).

Real part: \(\operatorname{Re}(z)=x\).

Imaginary part: \(\operatorname{Im}(z)=y\).

Equality: \(a+bi=c+di\) exactly when \(a=c\) and \(b=d\).

Argand diagram: \(z=x+iy\) is represented by the point \((x,y)\).

Powers of \(i\): \(i,-1,-i,1\), then the cycle repeats every four powers.

02
PART TWO

Modulus and Argument

Describe a complex number using its distance and direction.

1. The modulus of a complex number

The modulus of a complex number is the distance of its point from the origin on the Argand diagram. It is always non-negative.

For \(z=x+iy\), Pythagoras' theorem gives:

\[\boxed{|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\]
The modulus is the length of the line segment joining the origin to the point \(z\).

Example

Find the modulus of \(z=3+4i\).

\[\begin{aligned} |z|&=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+16}\\ &=\boxed{5} \end{aligned}\]

2. The argument of a complex number

The argument of a non-zero complex number is the angle measured from the positive real axis to the line joining the origin to the point representing the complex number.

  • Anticlockwise rotation is positive.
  • Clockwise rotation is negative.
  • Arguments in this lesson are measured in radians.
\[\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)\]

The principal argument is conventionally chosen in the interval:

\[\boxed{-\pi<\operatorname{Arg}(z)\leq\pi}\]
! PAY ATTENTION

Always check the range mentioned in the exam question. If a question specifies a different interval for the argument, use the interval given in that question.

The standard principal range includes \(+\pi\) but excludes \(-\pi\), because these two angles point in exactly the same direction. Only one endpoint is included.

The argument of \(z=0\) is undefined, because the zero complex number has no direction from the origin.

3. Finding the argument in each quadrant

First find the acute reference angle using the positive magnitudes of the real and imaginary components:

\[\alpha=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right),\qquad x>0,\ y>0\]

The signs of the real and imaginary parts tell us the quadrant. The argument must then be adjusted according to that quadrant.

QUADRANT I \(z=x+iy\)
\[\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac yx\right)\]
QUADRANT II \(z=-x+iy\)
\[\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)=\pi-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac yx\right)\]
QUADRANT III \(z=-x-iy\)
\[\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)=-\pi+\tan^{-1}\left(\frac yx\right)\]
QUADRANT IV \(z=x-iy\)
\[\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac yx\right)\]
R REMEMBER

The inverse tangent gives the reference angle. It does not automatically identify the correct quadrant. Always inspect the signs of the real and imaginary parts before choosing the argument formula.

4. Worked examples

Example 1: \(z=-3+3i\) (Quadrant II)

Both component magnitudes are 3, so the reference angle is:

\[\alpha=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac33\right)=\frac{\pi}{4}\]

Since the point is in Quadrant II:

\[\operatorname{Arg}(z)=\pi-\frac{\pi}{4}=\boxed{\frac{3\pi}{4}}\]

Example 2: \(z=-3-3i\) (Quadrant III)

The reference angle is again \(\frac{\pi}{4}\). Since the point is in Quadrant III, the principal argument is negative:

\[\operatorname{Arg}(z)=-\pi+\frac{\pi}{4}=\boxed{-\frac{3\pi}{4}}\]

5. Special cases on the axes

If a complex number lies directly on one of the axes, use its position rather than the quadrant formulas.

Positive real axis

\[z=x,\ x>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\operatorname{Arg}(z)=0\]

Positive imaginary axis

\[z=iy,\ y>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\operatorname{Arg}(z)=\frac{\pi}{2}\]

Negative real axis

\[z=-x,\ x>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\operatorname{Arg}(z)=\pi\]

Negative imaginary axis

\[z=-iy,\ y>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\operatorname{Arg}(z)=-\frac{\pi}{2}\]
! REMEMBER THE ORIGIN

For \(z=0\), the argument is undefined. The origin has no direction.

✓ KEY INFORMATION • PART 2

Modulus: \(|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), the distance from the origin.

Argument: the angle measured from the positive real axis.

Direction: anticlockwise is positive; clockwise is negative.

Principal argument: \(-\pi<\operatorname{Arg}(z)\leq\pi\).

Quadrants: identify the signs of the real and imaginary parts before selecting the formula.

Special case: the argument of zero is undefined.
