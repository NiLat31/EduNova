Part 1

Understanding Complex Numbers

Before exploring complex numbers, we need to understand why mathematicians introduced a new type of number and how it extends the real number system.

1. Why do we need complex numbers?

Consider the following quadratic equation:

\[ x^2+6x+13=0 \]

Using the discriminant:

\[ \Delta=b^2-4ac=6^2-4(1)(13)=-16 \]

Since the discriminant is negative, the equation has no real roots. This leads us to introduce a new number, called the imaginary unit.

The imaginary unit The imaginary unit is represented by \(i\), where:
\[ \boxed{i=\sqrt{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad i^2=-1} \]

2. Square roots of negative numbers

For example:

\[ \sqrt{-49}=\sqrt{49}\sqrt{-1}=7i \]
Pay attention The principal square root of \(-49\) is \(7i\). However, the equation \(z^2=-49\) has two solutions: \[ z=\pm 7i \]

3. Powers of the imaginary unit

The powers of \(i\) follow a repeating cycle. Let's calculate the first four powers:

\[ \begin{aligned} i^1&=i\\ i^2&=-1\\ i^3&=-i\\ i^4&=1 \end{aligned} \]
Remember The powers of \(i\) repeat every four powers: \[ i,\;-1,\;-i,\;1 \]

To calculate a large power of \(i\), divide its exponent by \(4\) and use the remainder.

Example 1
Evaluate \(i^{2026}\).
\[ 2026=4(506)+2 \] \[ i^{2026}=i^2=\boxed{-1} \]
Example 2
Evaluate \(i^{537}\).
\[ 537=4(134)+1 \] \[ i^{537}=i^1=\boxed{i} \]
Important When dividing the exponent by \(4\), use the remainder, not the quotient.

4. The general form of a complex number

A complex number is a number that can be expressed in the form:

\[ \boxed{z=x+iy} \]
  • \(x\) is the real part of the complex number.
  • \(y\) is the imaginary part of the complex number.
  • \(i\) is the imaginary unit.
\[ \operatorname{Re}(z)=x \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=y \]
Remember The imaginary part is the coefficient of \(i\), not the entire term \(iy\). For example, if \(z=5-3i\), then: \[ \operatorname{Re}(z)=5,\qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=-3 \]

5. Equality of complex numbers

Two complex numbers are equal if and only if their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal.

\[ a+bi=c+di \] \[ \boxed{a=c\quad\text{and}\quad b=d} \]
Example
If \(z_1=4+7i\) and \(z_2=a+bi\), and \(z_1=z_2\), find \(a\) and \(b\).
\[ \boxed{a=4,\qquad b=7} \]

Modulus and Argument

6. The modulus of a complex number

The modulus of a complex number is the distance between its point on the Argand diagram and the origin.

\[ \boxed{|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} \]

This formula comes directly from Pythagoras' theorem.

Example
Find the modulus of \(z=3+4i\).
\[ |z|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2} \] \[ |z|=\sqrt{9+16}=\boxed{5} \]

7. The argument of a complex number

The argument of a complex number is the angle measured from the positive real axis to the line joining the origin to the point representing the complex number.

We use the notation \(\operatorname{Arg}(z)\) for the principal argument. Positive angles are measured anticlockwise, while negative angles are measured clockwise.

Remember The principal argument is defined in the interval: \[ \boxed{-\pi < \operatorname{Arg}(z)\leq \pi} \] The argument of \(z=0\) is undefined.

8. Direction of angles in the four quadrants

The direction of the angle depends on the quadrant in which the complex number lies. Select a quadrant below to see its angle drawn on the Argand diagram.

Interactive Quadrant Selector

Select one quadrant at a time. The diagram will show the direction of the angle from the positive real axis.

Quadrant I

Both the real and imaginary parts are positive.

\[ \theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z) =\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) \]
Pay attention In the quadrant formulas below, \(x\) and \(y\) represent positive magnitudes. The signs of the real and imaginary parts are shown separately.

9. Argument formulas in each quadrant

Quadrant I
\[ z=x+iy \] \[ \boxed{\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z) =\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)} \] The angle is positive and measured anticlockwise.
Quadrant II
\[ z=-x+iy \] \[ \boxed{\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z) =\pi-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)} \] The angle is positive and measured anticlockwise.
Quadrant III
\[ z=-x-iy \] \[ \boxed{\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z) =-\pi+\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)} \] The principal angle is negative and measured clockwise.
Quadrant IV
\[ z=x-iy \] \[ \boxed{\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z) =-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)} \] The angle is negative and measured clockwise.

10. Worked examples

Example 1
Find the principal argument of \(z=-3+3i\).

The point lies in Quadrant II.

\[ \theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z) =\pi-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{3}\right) \] \[ =\pi-\frac{\pi}{4} =\boxed{\frac{3\pi}{4}} \]
Example 2
Find the principal argument of \(z=-3-3i\).

The point lies in Quadrant III.

\[ \theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z) =-\pi+\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3}{3}\right) \] \[ =-\pi+\frac{\pi}{4} =\boxed{-\frac{3\pi}{4}} \]

11. Arguments on the axes

When a complex number lies directly on one of the coordinate axes, we do not use the quadrant formulas.

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{Position} & \operatorname{Arg}(z)\\ \hline \text{Positive real axis} & 0\\ \text{Positive imaginary axis} & \frac{\pi}{2}\\ \text{Negative real axis} & \pi\\ \text{Negative imaginary axis} & -\frac{\pi}{2} \end{array} \]
Important The complex number \(z=0\) has no defined argument because it does not have a direction from the origin.
