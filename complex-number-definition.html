CHAPTER 09 · COMPLEX NUMBERS

Definition of a Complex Number

Discover the imaginary unit, explore the algebraic form of complex numbers, and understand how they are represented on the complex plane.

Lesson 9.1 · Foundations

A new number system.

Complex numbers extend the real number system by introducing the imaginary unit. They allow us to work with square roots of negative numbers and represent numbers geometrically on the complex plane.

01

The imaginary unit

We define the imaginary unit \(i\) as the number whose square is equal to \(-1\).

The fundamental definition
\[\boxed{i^2=-1}\]

Definition

The imaginary unit is denoted by \(i\), where \(i=\sqrt{-1}\). It allows us to express square roots of negative real numbers.

For example, instead of leaving \(\sqrt{-9}\) in radical form, we can write:

\[\sqrt{-9}=\sqrt{9}\sqrt{-1}=3i\]

Pay attention

When simplifying a square root of a negative number, separate the negative sign first. For example, \(\sqrt{-16}=4i\), not \(-4\).

02

Algebraic form of a complex number

A complex number is written in the form \(a+bi\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers.

Standard form
\[\boxed{z=a+bi}\]

Real part

The real part is \(a\), written as \(\operatorname{Re}(z)=a\).

Imaginary part

The imaginary part is \(b\), written as \(\operatorname{Im}(z)=b\). It is the coefficient of \(i\).

Keep in mind

The imaginary part is the real coefficient \(b\), not the term \(bi\). For \(z=5-3i\), the real part is \(5\) and the imaginary part is \(-3\).

Worked example

For \(z=-4+7i\):

\[\operatorname{Re}(z)=-4,\qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=7\]
03

Powers of the imaginary unit

Using \(i^2=-1\), higher powers of \(i\) can be simplified. Notice that the values repeat in a cycle of four.

The repeating cycle
\[i,\quad -1,\quad -i,\quad 1,\quad i,\quad -1,\quad -i,\quad 1,\ldots\]
PowerValuePowerValue
\(i^1\)\(i\)\(i^5\)\(i\)
\(i^2\)\(-1\)\(i^6\)\(-1\)
\(i^3\)\(-i\)\(i^7\)\(-i\)
\(i^4\)\(1\)\(i^8\)\(1\)

Keep in mind

The powers repeat every four. Therefore, the value of \(i^n\) depends on the remainder when \(n\) is divided by \(4\).

Worked example

Simplify \(i^{2026}\).

\[2026=4(506)+2\]

The remainder is \(2\), so:

\[\boxed{i^{2026}=i^2=-1}\]

Alternatively, \(i^{2024}\times i^2=1\times(-1)=-1\).

04

Modulus of a complex number

A complex number \(z=a+bi\) can be represented by the point \((a,b)\) on the complex plane. Its modulus is the distance of this point from the origin.

Modulus formula
\[\boxed{|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}\]
A complex number on the Argand plane
The horizontal axis represents the real part and the vertical axis represents the imaginary part.
For \(z=3+4i\), the point is \((3,4)\) and its distance from the origin is \(5\).

Worked example

Find the modulus of \(z=3+4i\).

\[|z|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=\boxed{5}\]
05

Argument of a complex number

The argument of a non-zero complex number is the directed angle measured from the positive real axis to the line segment joining the origin to the point representing the complex number.

Principal argument
\[\boxed{\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(z)}\]
\[-\pi<\theta\leq\pi\]

Pay attention

In complex numbers, arguments are expressed in radians unless the question states otherwise. The principal argument is taken in the interval \((-\pi,\pi]\).

For a point \(z=a+bi\), the reference angle can be related to the horizontal and vertical components. The quadrant determines the correct argument.

06

Complex numbers in the four quadrants

The signs of the real and imaginary parts determine the quadrant. The argument is measured from the positive real axis, anticlockwise for positive angles and clockwise for negative angles.

Explore the four quadrants
Select a quadrant to see the position, algebraic form, and principal argument range.
Quadrant I: \(z=x+yi\), with \(0<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}\).
The angle arc always starts at the positive real axis and follows the shorter directed rotation to the point.
Algebraic forms by quadrant
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{I: }&z=x+yi\\ \text{II: }&z=-x+yi\\ \text{III: }&z=-x-yi\\ \text{IV: }&z=x-yi \end{aligned} \]

Keep in mind

Here \(x\) and \(y\) represent positive magnitudes. The signs in the algebraic form indicate the quadrant in which the complex number lies.

07

Bringing the ideas together

Worked example

Consider \(z=-3+4i\). Identify its real part, imaginary part, modulus, and quadrant.

Real part: \(\operatorname{Re}(z)=-3\).

Imaginary part: \(\operatorname{Im}(z)=4\).

Modulus:

\[|z|=\sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=5\]

Quadrant: The real part is negative and the imaginary part is positive, so \(z\) lies in Quadrant II.

Common mistake

Do not identify the quadrant using the argument alone without checking the signs of the real and imaginary parts. Also remember that the argument is measured from the positive real axis, not from the vertical axis.
