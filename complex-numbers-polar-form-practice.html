Polar Form Practice

Build fluency, connect different representations, and tackle problems where the angle is the real test. Try each question before opening its worked solution.

Before you begin: Unless a question says otherwise, give arguments in the principal interval \((-\pi,\pi]\). Remember that this interval includes \(\pi\), but excludes \(-\pi\). Give exact answers where possible.
01

Changing the representation

Foundation

For each complex number below:

  1. Find its modulus.
  2. Find its principal argument.
  3. Write it in polar form.
  4. Write it in exponential form.
  1. \(z_1=1+\sqrt3\,i\)
  2. \(z_2=-\sqrt3+i\)
  3. \(z_3=-2-2i\)
  4. \(z_4=5-5\sqrt3\,i\)
Show your work and answers

(a) \(z_1=1+\sqrt3\,i\)

Step 1: Identify the coordinates. Compare \(z=x+iy\) with \(1+\sqrt3 i\).

\[x=1,\qquad y=\sqrt3\]

Step 2: Find the modulus.

\[r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\]
\[r=\sqrt{1^2+(\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt{1+3}=\sqrt4=2\]

Step 3: Find the quadrant and argument. Both coordinates are positive, so the point is in Quadrant I.

\[\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}=\frac{\sqrt3}{1}=\sqrt3\]
\[\theta=\frac{\pi}{3}\]

Step 4: Write both forms.

Polar form\[z_1=2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{3}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\]Exponential form\[z_1=2e^{i\pi/3}\]

(b) \(z_2=-\sqrt3+i\)

Step 1: Identify the coordinates.

\[x=-\sqrt3,\qquad y=1\]

Step 2: Find the modulus.

\[r=\sqrt{(-\sqrt3)^2+1^2}=\sqrt{3+1}=2\]

Step 3: Find the quadrant. The real part is negative and the imaginary part is positive, so the point is in Quadrant II.

\[\tan\alpha=\left|\frac{y}{x}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\]
\[\alpha=\frac{\pi}{6}\]
\[\theta=\pi-\alpha=\pi-\frac{\pi}{6}=\frac{5\pi}{6}\]
Polar form\[z_2=2\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\]Exponential form\[z_2=2e^{i5\pi/6}\]

(c) \(z_3=-2-2i\)

Step 1: Identify the coordinates.

\[x=-2,\qquad y=-2\]

Step 2: Find the modulus.

\[r=\sqrt{(-2)^2+(-2)^2}=\sqrt{4+4}=\sqrt8=2\sqrt2\]

Step 3: Find the quadrant and reference angle. Both coordinates are negative, so the point is in Quadrant III. The reference angle is \(\pi/4\).

\[\theta=-\pi+\frac{\pi}{4}=-\frac{3\pi}{4}\]

This is in the required interval \((-\pi,\pi]\).

Polar form\[z_3=2\sqrt2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)+i\sin\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)\right)\]Exponential form\[z_3=2\sqrt2e^{-i3\pi/4}\]

(d) \(z_4=5-5\sqrt3\,i\)

Step 1: Identify the coordinates.

\[x=5,\qquad y=-5\sqrt3\]

Step 2: Find the modulus.

\[r=\sqrt{5^2+(-5\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt{25+75}=\sqrt{100}=10\]

Step 3: Find the quadrant and argument. The point is in Quadrant IV.

\[\tan\alpha=\left|\frac{-5\sqrt3}{5}\right|=\sqrt3\quad\Rightarrow\quad\alpha=\frac{\pi}{3}\]
\[\theta=-\alpha=-\frac{\pi}{3}\]
Polar form\[z_4=10\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)+i\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\right)\]Exponential form\[z_4=10e^{-i\pi/3}\]
02

Reading polar form backwards

Foundation

Each complex number is given in polar or exponential form. For each one:

  1. State its modulus and argument.
  2. Convert it into the form \(x+iy\).
  3. State the quadrant of its Argand point, where applicable.
  1. \(w_1=6\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\)
  2. \(w_2=4e^{-i\pi/4}\)
  3. \(w_3=3e^{i7\pi/4}\)
Show your work and answers

(a) \(w_1=6(\cos\frac{5\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{5\pi}{6})\)

The modulus is the number outside the brackets, and the argument is the angle inside.

\[r=6,\qquad\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}\]

Use \(x=r\cos\theta\) and \(y=r\sin\theta\).

\[x=6\cos\frac{5\pi}{6}=6\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=-3\sqrt3\]
\[y=6\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}=6\left(\frac12\right)=3\]
Answer\[w_1=-3\sqrt3+3i\]

The real part is negative and the imaginary part is positive, so the point is in Quadrant II.

(b) \(w_2=4e^{-i\pi/4}\)

First rewrite the exponential form as polar form using \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\).

\[w_2=4\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+i\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right)\]

Now calculate the real part.

\[x=4\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=4\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=2\sqrt2\]

Then calculate the imaginary coefficient.

\[y=4\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=4\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=-2\sqrt2\]
Answer\[w_2=2\sqrt2-2\sqrt2\,i\]

The point is in Quadrant IV. Its modulus is \(4\), and its argument is \(-\pi/4\).

(c) \(w_3=3e^{i7\pi/4}\)

The modulus is \(3\). The displayed angle \(7\pi/4\) is greater than \(\pi\), so convert it to the principal interval by subtracting \(2\pi\).

\[\theta=\frac{7\pi}{4}-2\pi=\frac{7\pi}{4}-\frac{8\pi}{4}=-\frac{\pi}{4}\]

Now convert to Cartesian form.

\[x=3\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\]
\[y=3\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\]
Answer\[w_3=\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}-\frac{3\sqrt2}{2}i\]

The point is in Quadrant IV. Its principal argument is \(-\pi/4\), not \(7\pi/4\).

03

Products and quotients

Core application

Let

\[z_1=3\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}\right),\qquad z_2=2\left(\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}+i\sin\frac{2\pi}{3}\right).\]
  1. Find \(z_1z_2\) in polar form, giving its argument in \((-\pi,\pi]\).
  2. Find \(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\) in polar form, giving its argument in \((-\pi,\pi]\).
  3. Explain why the product and quotient have different modulus calculations.
Show your work and answers

(a) Find \(z_1z_2\)

Step 1: Multiply the moduli.

\[r=3\times2=6\]

Step 2: Add the arguments.

\[\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}+\frac{2\pi}{3}\]

Use a common denominator of \(6\).

\[\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}+\frac{4\pi}{6}=\frac{9\pi}{6}=\frac{3\pi}{2}\]

Step 3: Adjust to the principal interval. Since \(3\pi/2>\pi\), subtract \(2\pi\).

\[\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}-2\pi=\frac{3\pi}{2}-\frac{4\pi}{2}=-\frac{\pi}{2}\]
Answer\[z_1z_2=6\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)+i\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right)=6e^{-i\pi/2}\]

(b) Find \(\dfrac{z_1}{z_2}\)

Step 1: Divide the moduli.

\[r=\frac{3}{2}\]

Step 2: Subtract the arguments in numerator-minus-denominator order.

\[\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}-\frac{2\pi}{3}\]
\[\theta=\frac{5\pi}{6}-\frac{4\pi}{6}=\frac{\pi}{6}\]

Step 3: Check the interval. Since \(-\pi<\pi/6\leq\pi\), no adjustment is needed.

Answer\[\frac{z_1}{z_2}=\frac32\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac32e^{i\pi/6}\]

(c) Explain the modulus rules

When multiplying, the lengths scale together, so the new modulus is the product \(r_1r_2\). When dividing, one length is scaled by the reciprocal of the other, so the new modulus is the quotient \(r_1/r_2\). The denominator must be non-zero.

04

Working directly with exponential form

Core application

Given

\[a=4e^{i7\pi/6},\qquad b=3e^{i5\pi/4},\]
  1. Find \(ab\) in exponential form with its argument in \((-\pi,\pi]\).
  2. Find \(a/b\) in exponential form with its argument in \((-\pi,\pi]\).
  3. Convert your answer to part (b) into polar form.
Show your work and answers

(a) Find \(ab\)

Write the multiplication rule for exponential form: multiply the coefficients and add the angles.

\[ab=(4\times3)e^{i(7\pi/6+5\pi/4)}\]

Calculate the coefficient.

\[4\times3=12\]

Find a common denominator of \(12\) for the angle.

\[\frac{7\pi}{6}+\frac{5\pi}{4}=\frac{14\pi}{12}+\frac{15\pi}{12}=\frac{29\pi}{12}\]

The angle is greater than \(\pi\), so subtract \(2\pi=24\pi/12\).

\[\frac{29\pi}{12}-\frac{24\pi}{12}=\frac{5\pi}{12}\]
Answer\[ab=12e^{i5\pi/12}\]

(b) Find \(a/b\)

Divide the coefficients and subtract the denominator's angle from the numerator's angle.

\[\frac ab=\frac43e^{i(7\pi/6-5\pi/4)}\]

Use denominator \(12\) to subtract the angles.

\[\frac{7\pi}{6}-\frac{5\pi}{4}=\frac{14\pi}{12}-\frac{15\pi}{12}=-\frac{\pi}{12}\]

The angle is already in the required interval.

Answer\[\frac ab=\frac43e^{-i\pi/12}\]

(c) Convert to polar form

Replace \(e^{i\theta}\) with \(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\), keeping the same modulus and angle.

Answer\[\frac ab=\frac43\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)+i\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)\right)\]
05

Recovering an unknown angle

Reasoning

Two non-zero complex numbers have the forms

\[u=5e^{i\alpha},\qquad v=2e^{-i\pi/3},\qquad -\pi<\alpha\leq\pi.\]

It is known that the principal argument of \(uv\) is \(\pi/4\).

  1. Write an equation involving \(\alpha\) before adjusting the angle.
  2. Find the possible values of \(\alpha\) in the stated interval.
  3. Find \(u\) in exponential form.
  4. Find the modulus of \(uv\).
Show your work and answers

(a) Form the angle equation

When two complex numbers are multiplied, their arguments are added.

\[\arg(uv)=\alpha+\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=\alpha-\frac{\pi}{3}\]

The principal argument is given as \(\pi/4\). However, the unadjusted sum may differ from it by a whole multiple of \(2\pi\).

\[\alpha-\frac{\pi}{3}=\frac{\pi}{4}+2k\pi,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z\]

(b) Find \(\alpha\)

Add \(\pi/3\) to both sides.

\[\alpha=\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{3}+2k\pi\]

Use denominator \(12\).

\[\alpha=\frac{3\pi}{12}+\frac{4\pi}{12}+2k\pi=\frac{7\pi}{12}+2k\pi\]

Now use \(-\pi<\alpha\leq\pi\). The value with \(k=0\) is \(7\pi/12\), which lies in this interval. Any other integer value of \(k\) moves the angle outside it.

Answer\[\alpha=\frac{7\pi}{12}\]

(c) Write \(u\)

Substitute the value of \(\alpha\) into the given expression.

Answer\[u=5e^{i7\pi/12}\]

(d) Find the modulus of \(uv\)

Multiply the moduli.

\[|uv|=|u||v|=5\times2=10\]
Answer\[|uv|=10\]
06

The principal-argument boundary

Careful reasoning

For each angle below:

  1. Convert it to an equivalent angle in \((-\pi,\pi]\).
  2. State exactly how many multiples of \(2\pi\) you added or subtracted.
  1. \(\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\)
  2. \(-\dfrac{7\pi}{4}\)
  3. \(\dfrac{13\pi}{3}\)
  4. \(-\pi\)
  5. \(\dfrac{17\pi}{2}\)
Show your work and answers

(a) \(11\pi/6\)

This is greater than \(\pi=6\pi/6\). Subtract \(2\pi=12\pi/6\).

\[\frac{11\pi}{6}-2\pi=\frac{11\pi}{6}-\frac{12\pi}{6}=-\frac{\pi}{6}\]
Answer\(-\pi/6\), subtract \(2\pi\) once.

(b) \(-7\pi/4\)

This is less than \(-\pi=-4\pi/4\). Add \(2\pi=8\pi/4\).

\[-\frac{7\pi}{4}+2\pi=-\frac{7\pi}{4}+\frac{8\pi}{4}=\frac{\pi}{4}\]
Answer\(\pi/4\), add \(2\pi\) once.

(c) \(13\pi/3\)

Subtract \(2\pi=6\pi/3\) once.

\[\frac{13\pi}{3}-\frac{6\pi}{3}=\frac{7\pi}{3}\]

The result is still greater than \(\pi\), so subtract \(2\pi\) again.

\[\frac{7\pi}{3}-\frac{6\pi}{3}=\frac{\pi}{3}\]
Answer\(\pi/3\), subtract \(2\pi\) twice.

(d) \(-\pi\)

The interval is \((-\pi,\pi]\). The left endpoint is excluded, so \(-\pi\) is not allowed. Add \(2\pi\).

\[-\pi+2\pi=\pi\]
Answer\(\pi\), add \(2\pi\) once.

(e) \(17\pi/2\)

Subtract \(2\pi=4\pi/2\) repeatedly until the result is in the interval.

\[\frac{17\pi}{2}-\frac{4\pi}{2}=\frac{13\pi}{2}\]
\[\frac{13\pi}{2}-\frac{4\pi}{2}=\frac{9\pi}{2}\]
\[\frac{9\pi}{2}-\frac{4\pi}{2}=\frac{5\pi}{2}\]
\[\frac{5\pi}{2}-\frac{4\pi}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}\]
Answer\(\pi/2\), subtract \(2\pi\) four times.
Important endpoint detail: For the interval \((-\pi,\pi]\), an angle equal to \(-\pi\) must be changed to \(\pi\). Do not leave it as \(-\pi\).
07

One result, two routes

Multi-step challenge

Let

\[p=2\sqrt2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)+i\sin\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)\right),\qquad q=4e^{i5\pi/6}.\]
  1. Write \(p\) in exponential form.
  2. Find \(pq\) in exponential form, with its argument in \((-\pi,\pi]\).
  3. Convert \(pq\) into the form \(x+iy\).
  4. Check your Cartesian answer by multiplying the Cartesian forms of \(p\) and \(q\).
Show your work and answers

(a) Write \(p\) in exponential form

Use \(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}\). The modulus and angle stay unchanged.

Answer\[p=2\sqrt2e^{-i3\pi/4}\]

(b) Find \(pq\)

Multiply the moduli.

\[r=2\sqrt2\times4=8\sqrt2\]

Add the arguments.

\[\theta=-\frac{3\pi}{4}+\frac{5\pi}{6}\]

Use denominator \(12\).

\[\theta=-\frac{9\pi}{12}+\frac{10\pi}{12}=\frac{\pi}{12}\]

The angle is already in the principal interval.

Answer\[pq=8\sqrt2e^{i\pi/12}\]

(c) Convert \(pq\) into Cartesian form

Use the exact values of \(\cos(\pi/12)\) and \(\sin(\pi/12)\).

\[\cos\frac{\pi}{12}=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4},\qquad \sin\frac{\pi}{12}=\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\]

The real part is the modulus multiplied by the cosine.

\[x=8\sqrt2\left(\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\right)\]
\[x=2\sqrt2(\sqrt6+\sqrt2)=2\sqrt{12}+4=4\sqrt3+4\]

The imaginary coefficient is the modulus multiplied by the sine.

\[y=8\sqrt2\left(\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\right)\]
\[y=2\sqrt2(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)=2\sqrt{12}-4=4\sqrt3-4\]
Answer\[pq=(4+4\sqrt3)+(4\sqrt3-4)i\]

(d) Verify by Cartesian multiplication

First convert \(p\). Since \(\cos(-3\pi/4)=-\sqrt2/2\) and \(\sin(-3\pi/4)=-\sqrt2/2\):

\[p=2\sqrt2\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}-i\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)=-2-2i\]

Now convert \(q\). Its angle is \(5\pi/6\).

\[q=4\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+i\frac12\right)=-2\sqrt3+2i\]

Multiply the Cartesian expressions using distributivity.

\[pq=(-2-2i)(-2\sqrt3+2i)\]
\[=4\sqrt3-4i+4\sqrt3\,i-4i^2\]

Since \(i^2=-1\), the last term becomes \(+4\).

\[=4\sqrt3+4+(-4+4\sqrt3)i\]
Verification\[pq=(4+4\sqrt3)+(4\sqrt3-4)i\]

This matches the answer obtained by multiplying in polar form.

08

Investigating a condition

Extension

A non-zero complex number \(z\) has modulus \(r\) and principal argument \(\theta\), where \(-\pi<\theta\leq\pi\). Another non-zero complex number \(w\) has modulus \(s\) and principal argument \(\phi\).

It is given that

\[|zw|=24,\qquad \left|\frac zw\right|=\frac32.\]
  1. Form two equations involving \(r\) and \(s\).
  2. Solve the equations to find \(r\) and \(s\).
  3. Given that the principal argument of \(zw\) is \(-\pi/5\), and \(\phi=\pi/4\), find \(\theta\) in the principal interval.
  4. Write \(z\) in exponential form.
Show your work and answers

(a) Form the equations

The modulus of a product is the product of the moduli.

\[rs=24\qquad\text{(1)}\]

The modulus of a quotient is the quotient of the moduli.

\[\frac rs=\frac32\qquad\text{(2)}\]

(b) Solve for \(r\) and \(s\)

Rearrange equation (2) to make \(r\) the subject.

\[r=\frac32s\]

Substitute this expression into equation (1).

\[\left(\frac32s\right)s=24\]
\[\frac32s^2=24\]

Multiply both sides by \(2/3\).

\[s^2=24\times\frac23=16\]

Therefore \(s=\pm4\), but a modulus cannot be negative. So \(s=4\).

\[r=\frac32\times4=6\]
Answer\[r=6,\qquad s=4\]

(c) Find \(\theta\)

The argument of a product is the sum of the arguments, up to a multiple of \(2\pi\).

\[\theta+\phi=-\frac{\pi}{5}+2k\pi\]

Substitute \(\phi=\pi/4\).

\[\theta+\frac{\pi}{4}=-\frac{\pi}{5}+2k\pi\]

Subtract \(\pi/4\) from both sides.

\[\theta=-\frac{\pi}{5}-\frac{\pi}{4}+2k\pi\]

Use denominator \(20\).

\[\theta=-\frac{4\pi}{20}-\frac{5\pi}{20}+2k\pi=-\frac{9\pi}{20}+2k\pi\]

For \(k=0\), this lies in \((-\pi,\pi]\). Other integer values move it outside the interval.

Answer\[\theta=-\frac{9\pi}{20}\]

(d) Write \(z\) in exponential form

We found that the modulus of \(z\) is \(r=6\), and its principal argument is \(\theta=-9\pi/20\). Substitute these into \(z=re^{i\theta}\).

Answer\[z=6e^{-i9\pi/20}\]
09

The Hidden Relationship

Challenge

Two non-zero complex numbers \(z\) and \(w\) satisfy

\[|z|=|w|=2\] \[z+w=2\sqrt2\]

Given that \(z\) and \(w\) are distinct:

  1. Determine the Cartesian forms of \(z\) and \(w\).
  2. Express both numbers in polar form, giving their arguments in the principal interval \(-\pi<\theta\leq\pi\).
  3. Without directly calculating their Cartesian forms again, determine \(zw\) and \(\dfrac zw\). Give both answers in polar form.
  4. Hence determine \(\dfrac{z+w}{zw}\), expressing your answer in Cartesian form.
  5. A student claims that because \(|z|=|w|\), it follows that \(\arg(z)=\arg(w)\). Explain precisely why this reasoning is incorrect.
Show your work and answers

(a) Determine the Cartesian forms

Let

\[z=a+bi,\qquad w=c+di\]

Using the given condition \(z+w=2\sqrt2\):

\[(a+bi)+(c+di)=2\sqrt2\]

Collect the real and imaginary parts.

\[(a+c)+(b+d)i=2\sqrt2+0i\]

Equating real parts and imaginary parts separately gives:

\[a+c=2\sqrt2\tag{1}\] \[b+d=0\tag{2}\]

Rearrange equation (2).

\[d=-b\]

Since both complex numbers have modulus 2, use the modulus formula.

\[a^2+b^2=2^2\] \[a^2+b^2=4\tag{3}\]
\[c^2+d^2=2^2\] \[c^2+d^2=4\tag{4}\]

Substitute \(d=-b\) into equation (4).

\[c^2+(-b)^2=4\] \[c^2+b^2=4\tag{5}\]

Subtract equation (5) from equation (3).

\[a^2+b^2-(c^2+b^2)=4-4\] \[a^2-c^2=0\]

Factorise using the difference of two squares.

\[(a-c)(a+c)=0\]

From equation (1), \(a+c=2\sqrt2\), which is non-zero. Therefore:

\[a-c=0\] \[a=c\]

Substitute \(c=a\) into equation (1).

\[a+a=2\sqrt2\] \[2a=2\sqrt2\] \[a=\frac{2\sqrt2}{2}\] \[a=\sqrt2\]

Since \(a=c\):

\[a=c=\sqrt2\]

Substitute \(a=\sqrt2\) into equation (3).

\[(\sqrt2)^2+b^2=4\] \[2+b^2=4\] \[b^2=4-2\] \[b^2=2\] \[b=\pm\sqrt2\]

Recall that \(d=-b\). Therefore, the imaginary parts must have opposite signs.

\[ \boxed{z=\sqrt2+\sqrt2i} \] \[ \boxed{w=\sqrt2-\sqrt2i} \]

The two values may be interchanged.

Answer \[ z=\sqrt2+\sqrt2i,\qquad w=\sqrt2-\sqrt2i \] or vice versa.

(b) Express both numbers in polar form

First, consider \(z=\sqrt2+\sqrt2i\).

Calculate its modulus.

\[r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\] \[r=\sqrt{(\sqrt2)^2+(\sqrt2)^2}\] \[r=\sqrt{2+2}\] \[r=\sqrt4\] \[r=2\]

Calculate its argument using the tangent ratio.

\[\tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\] \[\tan\theta=\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}\] \[\tan\theta=1\] \[\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}\]

Both coordinates are positive, so the number lies in the first quadrant.

\[ \boxed{ z=2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} +i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right) } \]

Now consider \(w=\sqrt2-\sqrt2i\).

Calculate its modulus.

\[s=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\] \[s=\sqrt{(\sqrt2)^2+(-\sqrt2)^2}\] \[s=\sqrt{2+2}\] \[s=2\]

Calculate its argument.

\[\tan\phi=\frac{y}{x}\] \[\tan\phi=\frac{-\sqrt2}{\sqrt2}\] \[\tan\phi=-1\] \[\phi=-\frac{\pi}{4}\]

The real part is positive and the imaginary part is negative, so the number lies in the fourth quadrant.

\[ \boxed{ w=2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) +i\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right) } \]
Answer \[ z=2\operatorname{cis}\frac{\pi}{4} \] \[ w=2\operatorname{cis}\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \]

(c) Determine \(zw\) and \(\dfrac zw\)

First: Multiplication

When multiplying complex numbers in polar form, multiply the moduli and add the arguments.

\[|zw|=2\times2\] \[|zw|=4\]

Add the arguments.

\[ \arg(zw)=\frac{\pi}{4} +\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \] \[ =\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{4} \] \[ =0 \]
Answer \[ \boxed{zw=4(\cos0+i\sin0)} \]

Second: Division

When dividing complex numbers in polar form, divide the moduli and subtract the arguments.

\[ \left|\frac zw\right|=\frac22 \] \[ =1 \]

Subtract the arguments.

\[ \arg\left(\frac zw\right) =\frac{\pi}{4}-\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \] \[ =\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{4} \] \[ =\frac{2\pi}{4} \] \[ =\frac{\pi}{2} \]
Answer \[ \boxed{ \frac zw=\cos\frac{\pi}{2} +i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} } \]

(d) Express the result in Cartesian form

We are given that:

\[z+w=2\sqrt2\]

From part (c), we found that:

\[zw=4\]

Substitute these values into the required expression.

\[ \frac{z+w}{zw}=\frac{2\sqrt2}{4} \] \[ =\frac{2\sqrt2}{2\times2} \] \[ =\frac{\sqrt2}{2} \]

Since the result is real, its imaginary part is zero.

Answer \[ \boxed{ \frac{z+w}{zw}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+0i } \]

(e) Explain the student's mistake

The modulus represents the distance of a complex number from the origin, whereas the argument represents its direction from the positive real axis.

Two complex numbers can have the same distance from the origin but point in different directions.

In this question:

\[|z|=|w|=2\] \[ \arg(z)=\frac{\pi}{4} \] \[ \arg(w)=-\frac{\pi}{4} \]

The moduli are equal, but the arguments are different.

Conclusion \[ \boxed{ |z|=|w|\not\Rightarrow \arg(z)=\arg(w) } \]
