Definition of Vectors

Learn what vectors represent, how they are written, how they appear geometrically, and how vectors can be combined and compared.

Part 01

What Is a Vector?

A vector is a quantity that has both magnitude and direction.

Magnitude tells us how large the quantity is, while direction tells us which way it acts.

Vector = magnitude + direction

A vector is therefore different from a quantity that only tells us a numerical amount.

Scalar

A scalar has magnitude only.

  • Mass
  • Time
  • Temperature
  • Distance

Vector

A vector has magnitude and direction.

  • Displacement
  • Velocity
  • Acceleration
  • Force
Key idea The direction is an essential part of a vector. Two quantities can have the same magnitude but represent different vectors if their directions differ.

Vectors on a Graph

Geometrically, a vector can be represented by a directed line segment. The arrow shows the direction of the vector.

The vector starts at point A and ends at point B. The arrowhead indicates the direction from A to B.

How Are Vectors Written?

A vector can be named using a lowercase letter with an arrow above it, such as:

⃗a

If a vector starts at point A and finishes at point B, it can also be written using the two points:

AB⃗

The order is important. The notation AB⃗ means the vector travels from A to B.

Important Reversing the order reverses the direction. Therefore, BA⃗ points in the opposite direction to AB⃗.
Part 02

Addition of Vectors

Vectors can be added together to produce another vector called the resultant vector.

A useful way to add vectors geometrically is the head-to-tail method.

The Head-to-Tail Method

Suppose we have two vectors, a⃗ and b⃗.

To add them, place the tail of b⃗ at the head of a⃗.

The resultant vector starts at the tail of the first vector and ends at the head of the second vector.

Vector a⃗
Vector b⃗
Resultant
a⃗ + b⃗ = resultant vector
Key idea Vector addition combines both the magnitude and direction of the vectors. The resultant represents the overall displacement produced by the two vectors.

Example

Worked Example

A vector a⃗ moves a point 4 units to the right and 2 units upwards. A second vector b⃗ moves the point 3 units to the right and 1 unit upwards.

Step 1: Write the vectors
a⃗ = (4, 2)      b⃗ = (3, 1)
Step 2: Add corresponding components
a⃗ + b⃗ = (4 + 3, 2 + 1)
Step 3: Simplify
a⃗ + b⃗ = (7, 3)
Part 03

Types of Vectors

Vectors can be compared according to their magnitude and direction. Three important relationships are equal vectors, opposite vectors, and parallel vectors.

TYPE 01

Equal Vectors

Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and the same direction, even if they are in different positions.

TYPE 02

Opposite Vectors

Two vectors are opposite if they have the same magnitude but point in opposite directions.

TYPE 03

Parallel Vectors

Two vectors are parallel if they act along parallel lines. Their directions may be the same or opposite.

1. Equal Vectors

Two vectors are equal when they have the same magnitude and the same direction.

They do not need to start at the same point.

a⃗ = b⃗

2. Opposite Vectors

Opposite vectors have equal magnitudes but point in opposite directions.

b⃗ = −a⃗
Important The negative sign changes the direction of the vector, not its magnitude.

3. Parallel Vectors

Parallel vectors act along parallel lines. Their magnitudes do not have to be equal.

For example, one vector may be twice the size of another while remaining parallel to it.

b⃗ = 2a⃗

The two vectors have the same direction because one is a positive multiple of the other.

Lesson Summary

A vector has both magnitude and direction. Vectors can be written using a letter with an arrow or by naming their initial and terminal points. Vectors can be added to produce a resultant vector, and they can be compared as equal, opposite, or parallel vectors.

Vector → Magnitude + Direction
a⃗ + b⃗ → Resultant
Equal → Same magnitude + Same direction
Opposite → Same magnitude + Opposite direction
Parallel → Parallel lines
