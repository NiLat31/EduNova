INTEGRATION • TOPIC 08

Integration by Parts

A clear method for integrating products of functions by differentiating one part and integrating the other.

\[ \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du \]
DIFFERENTIATE ONE • INTEGRATE THE OTHER
THE BIG IDEA

Sometimes an integral contains the product of two functions, and substitution does not give us a simpler integral. Integration by parts gives us another way to handle these products. The method comes from the product rule for differentiation and allows us to transform one difficult integral into another, hopefully simpler, integral.

01 • WHY DO WE NEED INTEGRATION BY PARTS?

When substitution is not enough

Consider the integral

\[ \int x\sin x\,dx \]

This is a product of two different functions:

\[ x \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sin x \]

You cannot integrate each function separately and multiply the answers. In other words,

\[ \int x\sin x\,dx \neq \left(\int x\,dx\right) \left(\int\sin x\,dx\right) \]

Integration by parts is designed specifically for situations like this. Instead of trying to integrate the whole product at once, we deliberately split it into two parts.

02 • WHERE DOES THE FORMULA COME FROM?

The product rule is the starting point

You may already know the product rule:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}(uv) = u\frac{dv}{dx} + v\frac{du}{dx} \]

In differential notation, this can be written as

\[ d(uv)=u\,dv+v\,du \]

Now integrate both sides:

\[ uv = \int u\,dv + \int v\,du \]

Rearranging gives the formula we need:

THE INTEGRATION BY PARTS FORMULA
\[ \boxed{ \int u\,dv = uv-\int v\,du } \]

The entire method is built around this one formula.

03 • WHAT DO u AND dv MEAN?

Split the integral into two parts

When you see an integral such as

\[ \int x\sin x\,dx \]

we choose one part to become \(u\), and the other part to become \(dv\).

PART A

Choose \(u\)

Choose the part that you want to differentiate. After differentiating it, we call the result \(du\).

PART B

Choose \(dv\)

Choose the part that you want to integrate. After integrating it, we call the result \(v\).

\[ u \longrightarrow du \qquad\qquad dv \longrightarrow v \]

So the basic pattern is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{choose }u \rightarrow \text{differentiate }u } \]
\[ \boxed{ \text{choose }dv \rightarrow \text{integrate }dv } \]
IMPORTANT

\(u\) is the part you differentiate, while \(dv\) is the part you integrate. Choosing them in the wrong way can turn a simple integral into a much harder one.

04 • THE LIATE RULE

How do I know what to choose for \(u\)?

This is one of the most important decisions in integration by parts. Fortunately, there is a useful rule called LIATE.

LIATE • CHOOSE \(u\) FROM THE EARLIEST TYPE

When two or more different types appear in the integral, prefer the type that appears earlier in LIATE.

L
Logarithmic \(\ln x\)
I
Inverse Trigonometric \(\sin^{-1}x,\cos^{-1}x,\tan^{-1}x\)
A
Algebraic \(x,\ x^2,\ 3x+1\)
T
Trigonometric \(\sin x,\cos x,\tan x\)
E
Exponential \(e^x,\ 2^x\)
THE RULE TO REMEMBER
\[ \boxed{ L\rightarrow I\rightarrow A\rightarrow T\rightarrow E } \]

Choose \(u\) from the type that appears earliest in this order. The remaining part becomes \(dv\).

For example, consider

\[ \int x\cos x\,dx \]

Here \(x\) is algebraic and \(\cos x\) is trigonometric. Algebraic comes before trigonometric in LIATE, so choose

\[ \boxed{u=x} \qquad \boxed{dv=\cos x\,dx} \]

Choose \(u\) using LIATE

Before solving the integral, practise the most important decision: which part should become \(u\)?

LIATE TRAINER

Pick the correct \(u\)

Select the part that should be chosen as \(u\).

\[ \int x e^x\,dx \]

06 • THE METHOD

The integration by parts recipe

Once you have chosen \(u\) and \(dv\), follow the same sequence every time.

STEP 1

Choose \(u\)

Use LIATE to decide which part should be differentiated.

STEP 2

Choose \(dv\)

The remaining part, including \(dx\), becomes \(dv\).

STEP 3

Find \(du\)

Differentiate \(u\).

STEP 4

Find \(v\)

Integrate \(dv\).

STEP 5

Use the formula

Substitute everything into \(\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du\).

STEP 6

Simplify

If another integral by parts is needed, repeat the process.

07 • WORKED EXAMPLE 01

\(\int x\sin x\,dx\)

EXAMPLE 01
Algebraic × Trigonometric
STEP 1 • CHOOSE \(u\) AND \(dv\)

\(x\) is algebraic and \(\sin x\) is trigonometric. According to LIATE, algebraic comes first.

\[ u=x \qquad dv=\sin x\,dx \]
STEP 2 • FIND \(du\)
\[ du=dx \]
STEP 3 • FIND \(v\)

Integrate \(dv\).

\[ v=\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x \]
STEP 4 • APPLY THE FORMULA
\[ \int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du \]
\[ \int x\sin x\,dx = x(-\cos x) - \int(-\cos x)(dx) \]
STEP 5 • SIMPLIFY
\[ =-x\cos x+\int\cos x\,dx \]
\[ =-x\cos x+\sin x+C \]
FINAL ANSWER
\[ \boxed{ \int x\sin x\,dx = -x\cos x+\sin x+C } \]
08 • WORKED EXAMPLE 02

\(\int x e^{2x}\,dx\)

EXAMPLE 02
Algebraic × Exponential
STEP 1 • CHOOSE \(u\) AND \(dv\)

\(x\) is algebraic and \(e^{2x}\) is exponential. Algebraic comes before exponential in LIATE.

\[ u=x \qquad dv=e^{2x}\,dx \]
STEP 2 • FIND \(du\)
\[ du=dx \]
STEP 3 • FIND \(v\)

Remember that integrating \(e^{2x}\) requires dividing by the derivative of \(2x\), which is \(2\).

\[ v=\int e^{2x}\,dx = \frac12e^{2x} \]
STEP 4 • APPLY THE FORMULA
\[ \int x e^{2x}\,dx = x\left(\frac12e^{2x}\right) - \int \left(\frac12e^{2x}\right)dx \]
STEP 5 • INTEGRATE THE REMAINING TERM
\[ = \frac12xe^{2x} - \frac12\int e^{2x}\,dx \]
\[ = \frac12xe^{2x} - \frac14e^{2x} +C \]
FINAL ANSWER
\[ \boxed{ \int x e^{2x}\,dx = \frac12xe^{2x} - \frac14e^{2x} +C } \]
09 • WORKED EXAMPLE 03

\(\int \ln x\,dx\)

EXAMPLE 03
Logarithmic Function
STEP 1 • WRITE THE INTEGRAL AS A PRODUCT

Although there is only one visible function, we can write \(1\) as the other factor.

\[ \int\ln x\,dx = \int\ln x\cdot1\,dx \]
STEP 2 • CHOOSE \(u\) AND \(dv\)

Logarithmic functions come first in LIATE.

\[ u=\ln x \qquad dv=dx \]
STEP 3 • FIND \(du\) AND \(v\)
\[ du=\frac1x\,dx \]
\[ v=\int dx=x \]
STEP 4 • APPLY THE FORMULA
\[ \int\ln x\,dx = x\ln x - \int x\left(\frac1x\right)dx \]
STEP 5 • SIMPLIFY
\[ = x\ln x - \int1\,dx \]
\[ = x\ln x-x+C \]
FINAL ANSWER
\[ \boxed{ \int\ln x\,dx = x\ln x-x+C } \]
10 • WHEN ONE ROUND IS NOT ENOUGH

Sometimes integration by parts must be repeated

Some integrals contain a product such as \(x^2e^{2x}\). The first application of integration by parts makes the problem simpler, but another integration by parts is still required.

\[ \int x^2e^{2x}\,dx \]

Choose

\[ u=x^2 \qquad dv=e^{2x}\,dx \]

Then

\[ du=2x\,dx \qquad v=\frac12e^{2x} \]

Applying integration by parts:

\[ \int x^2e^{2x}\,dx = \frac12x^2e^{2x} - \int xe^{2x}\,dx \]

Notice something important: the new integral

\[ \int xe^{2x}\,dx \]

is exactly the type of integral we solved in Example 02. Therefore, we can apply integration by parts again.

THE PATTERN
  1. Choose \(u=x^2\), so differentiation reduces \(x^2\) to \(2x\).
  2. Apply integration by parts once.
  3. The remaining integral contains \(x\), which is simpler than \(x^2\).
  4. Apply integration by parts again.
  5. Continue until the algebraic factor has disappeared.
WHY THIS WORKS

When \(u\) is an algebraic expression such as \(x^2\), \(x^3\), or \(x^4\), differentiating it repeatedly eventually reduces it to zero. This is why choosing the algebraic factor as \(u\) is often so useful.

11 • COMMON TRAPS

Things to watch carefully

TRAP 01

Choosing \(u\) randomly. Always check LIATE first when the integral contains different types of functions.

BETTER APPROACH

Identify the types of functions and choose \(u\) according to the LIATE order.

TRAP 02

Forgetting that \(dv\) includes \(dx\).

BETTER APPROACH

Write \(dv\) with the differential, then integrate it to find \(v\).

TRAP 03

Forgetting the minus sign in \(uv-\int v\,du\).

BETTER APPROACH

Write the complete formula before substituting values.

TRAP 04

Forgetting the constant factor when integrating something such as \(e^{2x}\).

BETTER APPROACH

Differentiate your value of \(v\) mentally to check that it really gives \(dv\).

12 • QUICK CHECK

Before you solve an integration-by-parts question...

Remember these six things

  • Recognize the product: integration by parts is especially useful for products of functions.
  • Choose \(u\): use the LIATE order.
  • Choose \(dv\): the remaining factor, including \(dx\).
  • Differentiate \(u\): find \(du\).
  • Integrate \(dv\): find \(v\).
  • Use the formula: \[ \int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du \]
THE ONE FORMULA TO KNOW
\[ \boxed{ \int u\,dv = uv-\int v\,du } \]

And the one rule that helps you decide what to differentiate:

\[ \boxed{ \text{LIATE} = \text{Logarithmic} \rightarrow \text{Inverse Trigonometric} \rightarrow \text{Algebraic} \rightarrow \text{Trigonometric} \rightarrow \text{Exponential} } \]
